# Molecular Phylogenetic Analysis of Canidae using NCBI and Galaxy

## Project Overview
Phylogenetic analysis is a powerful tool for investigating evolutionary relationships among organisms using molecular data. This study analyzed the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI/COX1) gene sequences of six selected species within the family Canidae (_Canis lupus, Canis aureus, Canis latrans, Canis simensis, Lycaon pictus, and Nyctereutes procyonoides_) with _Felis catus_ serving as the outgroup. Nucleotide sequences were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database and processed using the Galaxy platform. Sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT, a consensus sequence was generated using the Consensus.seqs tool, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree. The resulting phylogenetic tree grouped all canid species into a single clade, indicating their shared evolutionary ancestry. _Canis aureus_ and _Canis lupus_ formed one pair of sister taxa, while _Canis simensis_ and _Canis latrans_ formed another. _Lycaon pictus_ was recovered as sister to the latter clade, whereas _Nyctereutes procyonoides_ diverged earlier within the canid lineage. The consensus sequence revealed both conserved and variable nucleotide sites, providing additional evidence of genetic similarities and differences among the selected taxa. Overall, the findings demonstrate the effectiveness of the mitochondrial COI gene and bioinformatics tools in reconstructing evolutionary relationships and support the use of molecular sequence data for phylogenetic studies within the family Canidae.

## Taxonomic Group
The in-group consists of species belonging to the family _Canidae_, which is taxonomically classified under Kingdom Animalia, Phylum Chordata, Class Mammalia, and Order Carnivora (_Suborder Caniformia_). This group is represented by _Canis lupus, Canis aureus, Canis latrans, Canis simensis, Lycaon pictus_, and _Nyctereutes procyonoides_. As dog-like carnivorans, these species share defining physical traits suited for endurance pursuit, such as non-retractile claws, deep-chested bodies, and elongated muzzles.
‎
‎The out-group is the family _Felidae_, represented by the domestic cat (_Felis catus_). While Felidae shares the identical higher taxonomic hierarchy which are the Kingdom Animalia, Phylum Chordata, Class Mammalia, and Order Carnivora. It is branches into the suborder Feliformia. Because _Felis catus_ shares a common carnivoran origin with canids but diverged before the dog family evolved its specialized features, distinct felid traits such as retractile claws and shortened muzzles serve as a baseline.


## Methods

Nucleotide sequences were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database by searching for each selected organism and specifying the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI/COX1) gene region. The corresponding NCBI accession numbers and sequence lengths in base pairs (bp) were recorded for reference. After retrieving all required sequences, the downloaded files were renamed according to their respective scientific names to ensure proper identification and organization of the datasets. 
The collected sequences were then uploaded into the Galaxy platform, where individual datasets were combined into a single file using the Concatenate Datasets tail-to-head (cat) tool. The concatenated dataset was then downloaded and manually edited to remove unnecessary header information, resulting in a cleaner sequence file for downstream analysis. The edited file was re-uploaded to Galaxy and subjected to multiple sequence alignment using the MAFFT (Multiple Alignment using Fast Fourier Transform) tool to generate aligned nucleotide sequences suitable for phylogenetic analysis.
To minimize the influence of sequencing errors and improve sequence comparability, the aligned sequences were further processed for consensus analysis. The MAFFT output file was converted from FASTA format into mothur.align format to ensure uniform sequence length and maintain co-linear alignment positions before running the Consensus.seq tool. Finally, the aligned dataset generated from MAFFT was used for phylogenetic tree construction through the FastTree tool, which produced the phylogenetic tree based on the evolutionary relationships among the selected taxa.

	The consensus sequence represents the most common nucleotide at each position in the multiple sequence alignment of the seven taxa. Variable sites contain phylogenetic information because they represent genetic differences that have accumulated throughout the entire evolution process. Species that share similar nucleotide substitutions are generally more closely related and are more likely to have inherited these changes from a common ancestor. Therefore, variable sites are useful for inferring evolutionary relationships among species.

Figure 4. This phylogenetic tree identifies the _Felis catus_ as the outgroup because it belongs to the family Felidae, providing a reference point for rooting the tree and distinguishing it from the ingroup. All canid species form a single clade, indicating that they share a common evolutionary ancestor (Senn et al 2022). Within this clade, _Canis aureus_  and  _Canis lupus_ form one pair of sister taxa, while _Canis simensis_ and _Canis latrans_ form another, indicating that each pair shares a more recent common ancestor compared to the other species. _Lycaon pictus_ is sister taxa to the _Canis simensis_ and _Canis latrans_ clade, whereas _Nycteruetes procyonoides_  diverges early within the same lineage. The branch lengths represent the amount of genetic change inferred from the COI/COX1 gene sequences – the shorter the branches between the taxa indicate lower genetic divergence and closer evolutionary relationships (Liang Liu 2015), while the longer internal branch separating _Canis aureus_ and _Canis lupus_ lineage from the remaining canids suggest greater genetic divergence and an earlier evolutionary split.

## Discussion
There is a broad consensus among researchers that COI is the most effective single marker for animal species identification. COI consistently distinguishes closely related species across all major animal phyla, including insects, crustaceans, arachnids,  mollusks, fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals, with a level of resolution that no other single gene has matched. It was also discussed that researchers can generate new COI sequences using standardized protocols and directly compare them without the need to design custom primers or optimize conditions for each new taxonomic group. Because COI sequences are relatively short and contain high interspecific variability, BLAST searches against NCBI’s nucleotides collection are computationally fast and typically return unambiguous top hits with high percent identity and coverage, making it more accessible, faster, reliable, and efficient than sequencing longer genes, multiple panel, or whole mitochondria genomes. Despite its widespread use, COI does not really work well across broad taxonomics especially in plants, fungi, and many protists since these organisms do not possess primer binding sites or show enough variation in COI to be useful— instead you will use a different marker. Another issue is that NCBI itself is not perfectly curated, there are plenty of sequences in the database that are misidentified or lacking proper voucher information. These are true for understudied groups where few verified reference sequences exist. Furthermore, COI is a single mitochondrial gene, which means it tells you about maternal lineage but nothing about hybridization, recent intergression, or population-level processes. The mitochondrial COI gene has several limitations and was encountered by the researchers as conducting the activity. First, the COI struggles to resolve deeper evolutionary relationships within the family, though it can reliably separate clades for both disciplines. Relying also on one single gene means the analysis has limited phylogenetic signal and is vulnerable to artifacts like long- branch attraction, especially when the outgroup is as distantly related as the domestic cat.
